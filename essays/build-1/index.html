“And just as horse fanciers do not take equal care for every specimen of the species, but are always choosing and selecting the best, and training, rearing and cherishing them above all others, so those who are above us put their brand, as it were, on the best of the herd, and think they deserve special guidance. They use no reins or halters, of course, but guide them by reason, using secret signs of which the common herd have no experience.” (Plutarch, 111)

In his collected essays, Plutarch repeatedly returns to the theme of the common man versus the intellectual and the inherent superiority of one over the other. His writings are transfixed on defining the difference between the passive soul and the active mind. Across his essays, Plutarch outlines the process by which one can train the mind, the spiritual mechanics behind human actions, and historical examples of philosophical success. Yet underlying this framework is a subtle psychological tactic: Plutarch preys on the reader's conscious dreams of greatness to motivate the common man to transcend his ordinary nature.

Plutarch explores the distinction between men by contrasting emotional volatility with logic, noting that the common man is easily swayed by the “intense pleasures” of the world (99). He delves into the specifics of the daimon through the myth of Timarchus. As Simmias recounts, some souls—the emotional, irrational ones—“sink entirely into the body” and lose the ability to access reason because they are overwhelmed by physical sensations (108). For men whose souls are not completely submerged, the daimon acts as an external element of consciousness capable of guiding intellect. Plutarch uses this structural understanding of the soul to advise his students who “perceive the struggle of virtue with circumstance, or the play of intelligent daring in the face of danger, when reason joins forces with the emotions of the moment” (83). He urges his students to become highly aware of their daimons and train their souls to be “made responsive to the rein” (109). Even in the presence of a daimon, vestiges of the disorderly mind remain, but the disciplined individual can leverage these divine resources. This marks the true divide between the ordinary man and the thinker: how they train themselves. By undertaking this effort, individuals are selected as superior to their peers, achieving a state of heightened consciousness where they are specially noticed by the divine. Through Epaminondas, Plutarch demonstrates how to begin training the intellect by suppressing the emotional soul; through Socrates, he illustrates the ultimate success story of a man who yields control to a higher power outside himself; and through Timarchus's myth, he synthesizes these ideas to explain the deeper meaning behind an intellectual's actions. Each of these tactics works to incentivize his reader.

To hammer his point home, Plutarch offers a final incentive to motivate the student. Because the intellectual soul is difficult to cultivate, it requires immense drive; thus, Plutarch plays into the reader's fundamental, emotional, desire to rise above the rest. Theanor, praising Simmias's account of the daimon, likens intellectuals to highly bred horses, explaining that the gods are always “selecting the best” to put their “brand” on “the best of the herd” (111). If a man refines himself through diligent training, he may be chosen by higher powers and granted access to a secret set of reins denied to the common herd. His daimon may grant him divine signs and subtle advantages far beyond facile material wealth. Plutarch implies that the thinker becomes superior on a level ordinary men can never reach: he transforms into a domesticated, highly trained creature yielding to a divine rider, achieving far more than an individual acting alone ever could.

Plutarch recognizes that even the best-trained men remain partially grounded in the flesh and he is not above appealing to this lower nature. This concept, as previously explored in Timarchus's myth, means that even the most logical men retain portions of their emotional soul and can be influenced by it. In including the horse training metaphor, Plutarch subtly acknowledges his own belief in the emotional soul's power and shallowly takes advantage of it. The strategy is intriguing given that philosophical men are typically portrayed as above such manipulation. Epaminondas, for instance, refuses to join a violent rebellion because he will not shed the blood of fellow citizens for personal gain. By appealing to the reader's emotional ego, is Plutarch similarly compromising his student's moral standards? He stokes the irrational, status-seeking parts of his students' conscious minds, leaving their initial motivation for pursuing intellect fundamentally flawed. While this ethical compromise may not affect every reader, it reveals Plutarch's ultimate pragmatic strategy: he is willing to motivate the common man by any means necessary, and in doing so affirms his belief in a two part consciousness. Any man can become an intellectual, even if it requires his irrational emotions to do so.

“As a matter of fact,” interjected my father, “I heard from a Megarian, who had heard it from Terpsion, that Socrates' daimonion was a sneeze, either his own or someone else's. If someone else sneezed on the right, either in front or behind, it persuaded him to do whatever he was about; if on the left, it dissuaded him from doing it. As to his own sneezes, one that happened while he was hesitating made him go forward; one that happened in the course of the act gave him pause and stopped his impulse. What seems amazing to me is that, if it was a sneeze, he did not admit it to his companions, but always said that the daimonion was stopping him or leading him on.” (Plutarch, 92)

Partway through Plutarch's dialogue, in the middle of a debate over what kind of thing Socrates' daimonion actually was, Caphisias' father breaks in with something he heard secondhand: “I heard from a Megarian, who had heard it from Terpsion, that Socrates' daimonion was a sneeze, either his own or someone else's” (92). This comes right after Galaxidorus has compared the daimonion to a small weight that only tips an already unsteady scale, something too slight to matter by itself. What he says he finds most amazing about it isn't the sneeze theory itself. It is that “if it was a sneeze, he did not admit it to his companions, but always said that the daimonion was stopping him or leading him on” (92).

The theory needs a surprising amount of fine print for something supposed to be random. Which side a sneeze came from mattered, in front or behind, right or left; his own sneezes needed a separate rule depending on whether they came “while he was hesitating” or “in the course of the act” (92). That is four different conditions built around one twitch of the nose. A real reflex should not need this many exceptions.

There is a name for this kind of explanation: confabulation, when someone acts for an ordinary reason, and their mind still hands them a story afterward, one that sounds completely sure of itself, that has nothing to do with the real cause. It is not lying. The person believes the story completely, because the actual reason was never available to them to report in the first place. If Socrates' daimonion really was a sneeze, something like this might be happening. An ordinary reflex, reframed each time into something with a will behind it. That would explain why the theory needs so much fine print: an explanation built to cover for something you do not understand tends to get more complicated than the real mechanism ever needed to be, because it has to account for every case instead of admitting there wasn't one. The story's thirdhand path fits too. It reached Caphisias' father through a Megarian and then Terpsion, and nobody in that chain claims to have heard it from Socrates himself. And it fits something the dialogue says right after, that a man who never wavered, not in exile, not at his own execution, would be an odd fit for someone whose decisions could be undone by “an accidental word or a sneeze” (92–93). If the sneeze theory is right, that consistency might never have been real. It could have been invented fresh each time, a twitch of the nose dressed up as conviction.

Later in the dialogue, Simmias offers his own account of the daimonion, arguing it was “not a vision but a perception of a voice” (104). Not a rumor here, and not passed through three sets of hands like the sneeze story. Something Socrates apparently felt, which Simmias trusts him to have reported honestly. Compare that to the sneeze. Same source, opposite conclusion. Simmias treats what Socrates said about his own mind as the starting point, the place you begin if you want to know what the daimonion actually was. The sneeze theory starts from that same kind of report and heads the opposite direction, treating it as something to be explained away instead of trusted. Nobody in the room stops to point out that these two arguments can't both be making the same assumption about whether a person knows his own mind.

Every theory raised in this room, sneeze included, assumes that what Socrates felt inside himself counts as evidence of something. Nobody disputes that part. The disagreement is only about what that something actually is. This passage comes the closest anyone gets to questioning the assumption itself instead of just building another theory on top of it, and then the conversation just moves on. Did Socrates actually have reliable access to his own mind, or did he do something far more ordinary: settle on a story he could live with because the real reason was never something he could see in the first place?

“The human soul, with all its countless puppet-strings of impulse, is by far the most sensitive machine of all. Handled according to reason, it quickly responds to any inclination and moves in whatever direction the mind conceives. In man, the strings of emotion and impulse terminate in the thinking mind. If this is disturbed, the strings are pulled, and in turn draw and pull upon the man” (Plutarch, 104–105)

A puppet is a motionless, inanimate object, unable to move without the influence of its strings. However, under a puppeteer's hand it moves, but only within the constraints of the puppeteer's movements. Plutarch uses this image to argue that emotion holds the same power over intellect that strings hold over a puppet. Human souls are governed by strings of impulse that are irrational, yet not uncontrollable. When those impulses take over, they disrupt our ability to be guided by reason or a higher power. However, the pull between emotion and reason runs both ways, and the mind can act back on emotion just as emotion acts on the mind. This reciprocity makes self-awareness possible, but only with a clear mind.
